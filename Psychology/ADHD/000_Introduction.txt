ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder)

1. What It Is (Clinical Definition)

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR) as a persistent pattern of:

Inattention

Hyperactivity

Impulsivity


that interferes with functioning or development.

It begins in childhood, but often continues into adolescence and adulthood.


---

2. Core Symptoms (Scientifically Classified)

ADHD has three presentations:

1. Predominantly Inattentive


2. Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive


3. Combined Presentation



A. Inattention (Examples)

Difficulty sustaining attention

Easily distracted

Forgetful in daily activities

Trouble organizing tasks

Avoids tasks requiring sustained mental effort


This is not laziness — it is a problem with executive functioning.


---

B. Hyperactivity

Fidgeting

Excessive talking

Difficulty staying seated

Feeling “internally restless”



---

C. Impulsivity

Interrupting others

Blurting out answers

Difficulty waiting turn

Acting without considering consequences



---

3. Brain Science (Neuroscience Evidence)

Research using fMRI and structural MRI shows:

Reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex

Altered functioning in basal ganglia

Differences in dopamine regulation


The prefrontal cortex controls:

Planning

Working memory

Impulse control

Attention regulation


ADHD is strongly linked to dopamine and norepinephrine dysregulation.

Think of dopamine as the brain’s “motivation and focus signal.”
In ADHD, this signal is weaker or inconsistently regulated.


---

4. Causes (Evidence-Based Understanding)

ADHD is not caused by bad parenting.

Major contributors:

1. Genetics (Most Significant Factor)

Heritability: ~70–80%

Runs strongly in families


2. Neurobiological Development

Delayed cortical maturation (2–3 years on average)


3. Environmental Risk Factors

Premature birth

Low birth weight

Prenatal exposure to alcohol or smoking

Severe early stress


No strong scientific evidence that:

Sugar causes ADHD

Watching TV causes ADHD



---

5. Prevalence (Global Data)

Children: ~5–7%

Adults: ~2.5–4%


It is diagnosed more often in boys during childhood, but in adulthood the gender gap narrows.


---

6. ADHD Across Lifespan

In Children

School difficulties

Behavioral issues

Academic underperformance


In Adolescents

Risk-taking behavior

Emotional dysregulation

Low self-esteem


In Adults

Job instability

Time management problems

Relationship difficulties

Chronic procrastination


Hyperactivity often decreases with age,
but inattention and executive dysfunction often remain.


---

7. Executive Function Impairment (Very Important Concept)

ADHD is better understood as a disorder of:

Self-regulation

Time management

Working memory

Emotional control


It is not a knowledge problem.
It is a performance regulation problem.

A person with ADHD may:

Know what to do

Intend to do it

Still fail to execute consistently


This distinction is critical.


---

8. Diagnosis (How It Is Done)

Diagnosis requires:

Symptoms present before age 12

Symptoms in two or more settings (home, school, work)

Functional impairment

Clinical assessment (not a blood test or brain scan)


Assessment includes:

Clinical interview

Behavioral rating scales

Developmental history



---

9. Treatment (Evidence-Based)

1. Medication (Most Effective for Core Symptoms)

Stimulants:

Methylphenidate

Amphetamine-based medications


They increase dopamine and norepinephrine availability.

Response rate: ~70–80%

Non-stimulants:

Atomoxetine

Guanfacine



---

2. Behavioral Therapy

Especially effective in children:

Parent training

Classroom structure

Skill training



---

3. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

Helpful for adults:

Time management

Organization

Emotional regulation



---

10. ADHD and Intelligence

ADHD is not related to intelligence level.

You can have:

High IQ + ADHD

Average IQ + ADHD

Learning disability + ADHD


They are independent variables.


---

11. Comorbidities (Very Common)

ADHD frequently co-occurs with:

Anxiety disorders

Depression

Learning disorders

Autism spectrum disorder

Substance use disorder


Up to 60–70% have at least one additional condition.


---

12. Long-Term Outcomes

Untreated ADHD is associated with:

Academic underachievement

Higher accident rates

Financial instability

Increased mental health risk


Early treatment improves:

Educational outcomes

Self-esteem

Functional stability



---

Mental Model Summary

ADHD =
Neurodevelopmental difference in executive control systems, primarily involving dopamine-regulated attention and impulse circuits.

It is a regulation disorder, not a willpower disorder.
